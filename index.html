The problem

One backbone for every modality — and one very expensive index

Universal multimodal embedders are a great deal: one model, one shared space, any query type. The bill arrives at serving time.

Storage

16 KB per vector

A single Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B vector is 4096 FP32 floats. Multiply by a 542K-image catalogue and the index alone is measured in gigabytes — before you add replicas.

Latency

7 minutes per exact scan

Exact dense scoring is $O(nd)$. On VisualNews (542K candidates) at 8B, one exhaustive pass takes 423.8 s. Approximate indexes help — at the cost of accuracy and a whole extra system to tune.

Retraining

Not an option

The obvious fix — train a sparse retriever from scratch — throws away the generality you paid for, and the compute bill for a multimodal backbone is enormous.

So the question PUMA asks is: can you sparsify a frozen embedder after the fact, and keep the rankings?

The idea

Keep the model. Rewrite the vectors.

PUMA sits after the embedder. Dense vectors go in; codes with exactly k active features come out. Nothing upstream changes.

Only k (feature id, weight) pairs per vector — typically 128–160 out of a learned dictionary of 32,768. Scoring is a sparse dot product over overlapping ids, so it reuses the machinery inverted indexes have been optimised for over decades.

Dense · 2048 floats · 8,192 B PUMA · k=128 · 656 B

The animation shows a 28-feature slice of the real 32,768-wide dictionary. TopK is a hard selection: everything outside the top k is discarded, so the code size is fixed and known in advance — no per-vector variance to budget for.

Stage ①

Cache once

Run the frozen backbone over your corpus a single time and keep the dense vectors. Everything after this is cheap and never touches the backbone again.

Stage ②

Learn the dictionary

Train a TopK sparse autoencoder that is explicitly taught to preserve the dense dot product — not just to reconstruct the vector.

Stage ③

Tune for ranking

Fine-tune the encoder contrastively using the exact sparse dot product you will serve with. Train-time and serve-time scoring finally agree.

Why accuracy holds

Three things a plain sparse autoencoder gets wrong

Reconstruction is not ranking. PUMA's recipe is three targeted fixes — and the ablations show all three are load-bearing.

Fix 1

Distil the dot product

A reconstruction loss optimises the wrong thing. PUMA additionally forces the sparse score to match the dense score for every query–candidate pair, so the ranking geometry survives compression.

−.036 nDCG@10 on CIRR when the related AuxK term is removed.

Fix 2

Force a shared vocabulary

Left alone, the dictionary splits: some atoms fire only for images, others only for text. Cross-modal scoring then compares two disjoint alphabets. PUMA penalises exactly that.

−.026 nDCG@10 on CIRR without the alignment loss.

Fix 3

Revive dead features

TopK only sends gradient to the k selected atoms, so the other 32,000+ quietly die and your dictionary shrinks to nothing. AuxK asks dead atoms to explain the leftover residual.

−.029 nDCG@10 on Fashion200K without the contrastive blend.

The full objective, loss weights, and leave-one-out ablations

Stage ② optimises five terms; Stage ③ switches the emphasis to ranking:

$$\mathcal{L}_2 = \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + \lambda_a \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} + \lambda_d \mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}} + \lambda_x \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} + \lambda_c \mathcal{L}_{\text{ctr}} \qquad \mathcal{L}_3 = \mathcal{L}_{\text{ctr}} + \alpha \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}$$

with $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\beta = 0.05$ in the main experiments.

  • Reconstruction — $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} = 1 - \cos(x, \hat{x})$, on both query and candidate embeddings.
  • Dot-product distillation — $\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}} = \big(g(q)^\top g(d) - q^\top d\big)^2$.
  • Cross-modal alignment — $\lVert m_{\text{img}} - m_{\text{txt}} \rVert_1$ over TopK support masks, plus magnitude alignment on co-active atoms. Gradients reach the selected pre-TopK activations through the same straight-through treatment as TopK itself.
  • AuxK feature revival — atoms inactive for more than $T$ steps reconstruct the residual $x - \hat{x}$ under a stop-gradient, so only dead atoms are updated by the term.
  • Contrastive blend — a small InfoNCE term already during pretraining.
  • Progressive $k$-annealing — start at a larger $k_{\text{init}}$, anneal linearly to the target $k_{\text{final}}$.

Your call

One dial: how small do you want it?

$k$ is set at inference, on the same checkpoint. Turn it down until the quality/storage trade stops being worth it.

Aggressive ~82 B

k = 16 · ~100× smaller than FP32 dense at 2B. Still keeps 74% of the dense score on CIRR and 69% on Fashion200K.

Balanced ~246 B

k = 48 · ~33× smaller. Reaches 96% of dense on CIRR and already exceeds dense on Fashion200K.

Default 656 B

k = 128–160 · 8–16× smaller (25× with compact ids). Quality saturates here — this is where the headline results live.

When it breaks

Two failure modes — and how to spot them before you ship

Post-hoc sparsification does not always work. The useful part is that the two ways it fails look completely different on the diagnostics, and both are measurable during training.

Failure mode 1 · Not enough support before TopK Qwen-8B · CIRR · $k = 160$
You ask for 160 active features…
…but the encoder only realises k on 81.8% of examples stalls

Only 166.5 positive pre-activations on average, against a requested $k = 160$. Raising $k$ cannot help — the support simply is not there to select from. Result: .484 sparse vs .496 dense nDCG@10.

Failure mode 2 · Plenty of support, pointing the wrong way RZenEmbed · VisualNews · $k = 160$
Support is abundant — 465.3 pre-activations, $k$ reached on every example
reach = 1.000 100%

And yet retrieval quality stays well below dense: .359 against .393 nDCG@10. The active atoms do not preserve the dense ranking geometry. Support availability is necessary, not sufficient.

That distinction is the practical takeaway: check reach before you tune $k$. If reach is below 1.0 you have a coverage problem and no amount of extra budget fixes it; if reach is 1.0 and quality still lags, you have an alignment problem. Both diagnostics can be monitored during training.

Under the hood

The parts we skipped

Everything a reviewer or an engineer reproducing this would want, kept out of the way.

Setup, backbones, and hyperparameters
  • Backbones (frozen). Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B ($d = 2048$) for the main experiments; Qwen3-VL-Embedding-8B ($d = 4096$) for within-family scaling; RZenEmbed ($d = 3584$) for cross-family transfer. Dense baselines and all sparse variants share identical prompts, cached embeddings, and candidate pools, so only sparsification varies.
  • Dictionary. Expansion ratio 16× throughout, so $D = 32{,}768$ for Qwen-2B and $D = 16d$ analogously for larger backbones. Varying $D$ is left for future work.
  • Optimisation. AdamW, weight decay $10^{-2}$; learning rates $3\times10^{-4}$ (Stage ②) and $1\times10^{-4}$ (Stage ③), with 1,000 / 500 linear warmup steps then cosine decay; 40,000 Stage ② steps and 5,000 Stage ③ steps; batch size 64; decoder column-norm renormalisation after every step.
  • Model selection. A fixed 5% of training queries (same seed for every run) held out for checkpoint selection by sparse nDCG@10; validation splits pick the operating point $k$; the test split is evaluated once.
  • Hardware. Dense embeddings extracted with vLLM where supported; sparse training and evaluation in PyTorch on a single NVIDIA A100.
  • Metrics. Hit@5, Recall@10, nDCG@10 — nDCG@10 used for validation.

Limitations we want to be upfront about
  • Main experiments centre on the Qwen embedding family, with RZenEmbed as a cross-family check. Transfer is real, but sparsification behaviour clearly depends on the geometry of the frozen backbone.
  • The failure analysis diagnoses two bottlenecks; it does not solve them. Future objectives should jointly encourage sufficient dictionary coverage and retrieval-aligned active features.
  • PUMA is post-hoc, so it inherits the representational properties of the frozen backbone — including any domain imbalance or bias. Deployment-sensitive settings should audit the sparsified retriever alongside the original dense model.

Four steps to a sparse index

No backbone retraining, one A100, and the embeddings you already computed.

💻 github.com/sisinflab/PUMA 📄 Read the paper
  1. Cache embeddings — run the frozen backbone once over your train / val / candidate partitions and store the dense vectors. Every later step reads from this cache, so sparse training cost never depends on the backbone.
  2. Stage ② — sparse pretraining — train the TopK SAE at $D = 16d$ with cosine reconstruction, dot-product distillation, cross-modal alignment, AuxK revival, the small contrastive blend, and progressive $k$-annealing to the target support.
  3. Stage ③ — retrieval fine-tuning — InfoNCE over sparse codes with in-batch (and hard, where available) negatives, keeping $\alpha \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}$ as regularisers. Select checkpoints on the 5% holdout by sparse nDCG@10.
  4. Evaluate & index — pick $k$ on the validation split, score the test split once with the sparse dot product, and optionally remap corpus-active atoms to compact ids for the 9-bit / 8-bit storage rows.
